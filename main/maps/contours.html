MAPS & PLACES / TERRAIN

Read a hill
through contour lines.

Topographic contours let a flat map describe the rises and dips of a landscape.

THE MAIN IDEA

Each contour joins places with equal elevation.

A contour line connects points that share an elevation relative to a reference level. The difference in elevation between adjacent contours is the contour interval, usually stated in a map legend. Closely spaced contours signal a steeper slope because the ground gains or loses elevation over a shorter horizontal distance; widely spaced contours indicate a more gradual slope. Contour shapes can reveal ridges and valleys. Where contours form a V pointing uphill, a stream valley often lies in the point of the V; the water typically flows the other direction, downhill. Closed loops may mark a hill, or a depression when special hachure marks indicate one. The pattern is a representation derived from survey data, not a direct view of every rock, cliff, embankment, or man-made cut. A small contour interval can communicate subtle terrain but may make a busy map difficult to read; a large interval simplifies the picture at the cost of detail. Elevation references vary: a map may use a national vertical datum that differs slightly from another country's reference. Do not compare elevations across datasets without checking the datum. The horizontal distance between contour lines is not fixed, so following a contour does not mean walking an easy or safe route; dense vegetation, cliffs, river crossings, fences, and erosion may not appear. Terrain data and maps have a publication date, grid size, and known accuracy, all of which matter for technical use. Hillshade can help you visualize form, but it can also make a ridge or hollow seem reversed depending on the assumed light direction. Contour lines remain a more explicit representation when read alongside elevation labels and the legend. For hiking, mountaineering, rescue, construction, or flood planning, use current official maps, local reports, proper training, and suitable equipment. A line on the page cannot replace weather awareness, navigation skills, or route judgment. Start by locating the contour interval, orienting the map, and tracing the spacing and shape around one landmark. Then compare the pattern with a safe public viewpoint or digital elevation model. A flat map becomes much more expressive once you think about how water would move across its slopes.

TRY THIS

Find the valley hidden in a V.

On a public topographic map, locate a stream and follow two contour lines across it. Note the direction each V points, then use elevation labels to confirm which way the ground rises. Check the contour interval before estimating any height difference.

Terrain safety: Map contours do not identify all hazards. Use current official navigation and local conditions.
