Follow a river
across the map.

THE MAIN IDEA

Tributaries merge into a drainage network.

A river network starts with small channels that collect runoff and groundwater, joining larger streams as they flow toward a lake, estuary, or sea. The smaller contributing channels are tributaries; their junction is a confluence. The land draining to a common outlet is a watershed or drainage basin. On a map, the pattern of tributaries can reveal terrain: channels tend to follow valleys, while ridges divide the directions water flows. Topographic contours are a useful companion, because contour V shapes frequently point toward higher ground as they cross a valley. A blue line alone cannot tell you the river's current depth, flow speed, water quality, seasonality, or whether it can be safely crossed. Rivers change with rainfall, snowmelt, reservoirs, drought, and human engineering. Dams, levees, canals, culverts, and urban drainage can alter a natural pattern. Historical maps may show channels that have been filled or straightened, while modern satellite images can hide water beneath tree cover or bridge structures. Map publication dates and data sources therefore matter. A river also connects people upstream and downstream: land use, industrial sites, agriculture, and stormwater can affect the water far from where a map reader is standing. Avoid treating an attractive blue route as a swimming or drinking recommendation. Water can be unsafe even when clear, and currents can be stronger than they appear. For field observation, stay on stable public paths and respect riverbank protections; do not enter channels or approach floodwaters. Compare a topographic map, a current aerial image, and local watershed agency information to understand the same place from different perspectives. Note where tributaries meet, where the floodplain widens, and how roads or settlements cross the water. Those features often indicate where the relationship between river and community deserves careful planning. A watershed map helps organize questions about water, but professional hydrology, flood modelling, and drinking-water testing require specialized methods and current measurements. Follow one tributary from headwaters to mouth and the apparent “line” becomes a system of connected landscapes.

TRY THIS

Follow a tributary upstream on a map.

Using an official public watershed or topographic map, find a familiar river mouth and trace a tributary toward its headwaters. Note confluences, elevation contours, and settlements. Do not use the exercise to choose a water source, cross a river, or approach a flood.

Water safety: Never enter floodwater or fast currents. Check official local water and flood guidance.
