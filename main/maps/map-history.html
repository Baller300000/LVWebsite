MAPS & PLACES / HISTORY

A map is also
a historical document.

Old maps preserve information about an era—and about the people who made and used them.

THE MAIN IDEA

A map reveals the priorities of its creator.

A historic map records far more than street positions. Its date and purpose tell you which places mattered to its maker: a sea chart emphasizes coastlines and navigable routes; a land survey values parcel boundaries; a propaganda map may use color and labels to support a political claim. Identify the cartographer, publisher, intended audience, and original format if those details are available. A map may have been redrawn, copied, or repaired long after its initial survey, and the date shown on the reproduction may not describe the underlying data. Compare the labels and boundaries with other sources from the same period. Place names change through language, migration, political control, and community usage. A historical name can reveal the mapmaker's perspective and may reproduce colonial or discriminatory terminology; repeating it without context can cause harm. Include both historical and current names only when they serve the research purpose, and explain the source. Blank spaces are not proof that no one lived there. Areas may be unrecorded in a particular archive, excluded from a map's purpose, erased by a political project, or beyond the mapping survey's method. The map's omissions therefore deserve attention alongside its details. Old scale, coordinate reference, and projection also affect whether the image can be aligned with present-day maps. Digitally georeferencing a historic map stretches it mathematically to fit selected control points, sometimes creating local distortions; an overlay is an interpretation rather than a perfect reconstruction. When tracing the evolution of a neighborhood, roads, rivers, and land parcels, cross-check archival map evidence with public records, photographs, oral histories, and published scholarship. Respect community knowledge and the sensitivities around Indigenous territories, cemeteries, and sacred sites. A map can be both valuable evidence and an instrument of governance, commerce, exploration, or dispossession. Asking who had the power to draw it and who could challenge that drawing makes cartographic history richer and more accurate. Archive catalogues often provide crucial metadata that a thumbnail omits. Cite the collection, shelf mark, date, and mapmaker when sharing a research conclusion, and make clear whether you are comparing a reproduced image or the original survey. A careful historical reading does not dismiss a map; it puts its information back into the circumstances that produced it.

Give an old map a source biography.

Find a publicly available archived map. Record its creator, date, commission or audience, area shown, projection if known, and two visible omissions. Compare one place name with a reliable modern source and preserve historical language in context rather than repeating it uncritically.

Respectful research: Historical maps may expose sensitive cultural or personal information; consult relevant communities and archival notes.
