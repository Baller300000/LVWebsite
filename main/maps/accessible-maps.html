MAPS & PLACES / ACCESS

Make a map useful
for different bodies.

A route is not accessible just because a line connects its endpoints.

THE MAIN IDEA

Access depends on details maps often omit.

A route that works for one traveller may be unusable for another because people move, perceive, and navigate in many different ways. A basic street map shows approximate paths but may omit surface quality, curb ramps, sidewalk width, cross slopes, steep gradients, steps, handrails, rest places, crossing times, signal type, accessible toilets, and construction closures. Public transit accessibility requires even more information: station lifts can be out of service, platforms have varying gaps, and vehicle boarding rules differ. A route planner can optimize for a generic walker while quietly sending a wheelchair user over stairs or a person with low vision across a crossing without tactile guidance. Conversely, an accessible path may be longer but still be the more useful option. Even “step-free” can be an incomplete label if a path is steep, narrow, or blocked by parked vehicles. Access needs vary within the same disability and may change day by day, so no single map can anticipate everyone's preferences. Inclusive wayfinding benefits from field audits, consistent symbols, user testing, and transparent information about what was not checked. Data should include the date and source because sidewalks, elevators, and entrances change. People who maintain a map should offer a clear method for reporting corrections without making access dependent on digital-only tools. Local organizations and disabled people themselves are essential experts; their experience should guide design and verification rather than be treated as an afterthought. If planning a real journey, contact the venue or transport operator for current details and discuss the route with the traveller whose needs matter. A map cannot decide another person's acceptable risk or effort. Don't infer what someone can manage from a diagnosis, photograph, or a generic accessibility symbol. In a map critique, ask which mobility devices or sensory needs were represented, who supplied the information, and whether an alternative is clearly identified. The goal is not to label every route “accessible” or “inaccessible” with false certainty, but to share concrete features so individuals can make informed choices. Better mapping makes uncertainty visible and recognizes that the person using the route is the authority on their own needs. Accessibility is an ongoing data and design practice, not a one-time icon placed on a basemap.

TRY THIS

Audit an everyday public route.

With permission and an appropriate participant, document a short route's surface, slope, steps, curb ramps, crossings, rest points, closures, and transit links. Record who checked the conditions and when. Do not describe the route as universally accessible based on a single inspection.

Travel planning: Verify current access directly with the venue, transit operator, and the people taking the trip.
