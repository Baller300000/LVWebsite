MAPS & PLACES / SYMBOLS

The legend is
part of the map.

Every symbol is a claim about how information has been grouped, measured, and displayed.

THE MAIN IDEA

Learn the visual language before reading the pattern.

A map legend explains how to interpret symbols, lines, colors, patterns, or marker sizes. A small blue line might represent a stream, a walking trail, a boundary, or a transit route depending on the map's key. Color can indicate discrete categories—such as types of land cover—or a continuous numerical range such as elevation or rainfall. Those are different visual encodings and should not be read in the same way. A sequential palette often progresses from low to high values, while a diverging palette emphasizes departures from a meaningful midpoint. A categorical map should use distinguishable symbols without implying a greater or lesser quantity unless its designer intends that. The range boundaries matter too: categories such as “0–10,” “10–20,” and “20–30” can leave the shared endpoints ambiguous unless the classification says which side includes them. A choropleth map colors whole areas by data rates, not necessarily by totals; a densely populated region with a moderate rate can have more people represented than a sparse area with a high rate. A proportional-symbol map scales symbol area, not diameter, if the intended quantity is the data value; confusing these makes large circles dramatically exaggerated. Read the units. Millimeters of rain and inches of rain cannot be compared directly, and a rate per household is not a count of all households. Check whether values are observed, estimated, interpolated, normalized, or missing. No-data regions are not zero. A legend may use a truncated color scale that exaggerates small differences, while a broad range can hide meaningful changes. Mapmakers make design choices to be legible, but the visualization's visual weight affects what readers notice first. Try reading the map description aloud: “this shade represents annual rainfall per unit area, averaged for these years.” If you cannot complete that sentence from the title, legend, and source note, the evidence needs more context. A proper legend should help identify both what a symbol means and the limitations of the data behind it. When comparing two maps, ensure classifications, units, dates, and projections match. Similar colors do not guarantee comparable quantities. These simple checks protect you from mistaking a neat visual pattern for a fair comparison or a precise measurement.

TRY THIS

Read one color as a sentence.

Choose a public data map and pick one colored region. Use its legend, title, units, and source to write exactly what the color means, including period and whether it shows a total, rate, estimate, or category. List anything those details do not answer.
