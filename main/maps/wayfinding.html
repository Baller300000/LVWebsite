MAPS & PLACES / FIELDCRAFT

Wayfinding:
check more than one clue.

Good navigation combines preparation, observation, up-to-date information, and willingness to change plans.

THE MAIN IDEA

A route is a decision made in a changing place.

Wayfinding is the process of figuring out where you are, where you intend to go, and which safe route connects the two. A map or phone is one source of information; landmarks, street signs, transit information, the sun, a compass, and local guidance can provide additional context. Relying on one clue is fragile. A familiar landmark might have been removed, a phone can lose power or satellite reception, and an apparent direction from the Sun depends on time, season, latitude, and weather. Before travelling, check the route, its date, entrance, operating hours, access requirements, and any known closures. For a hike or unfamiliar area, tell someone a realistic plan and expected return time, bring suitable clothing and water, check official weather and hazard alerts, and carry a backup navigation method appropriate to the terrain. The correct equipment and preparation depend on the location; a city walk and winter backcountry route do not have the same hazards. While moving, stop in a safe place to orient instead of staring at a screen beside traffic or an exposed edge. Compare the map's street shapes and landmarks with what you actually see. If observations conflict, reassess rather than forcing reality to match a stale route. At an intersection, note the name, direction, and next landmark before proceeding. Keep others' privacy and personal security in mind when displaying a map or sharing a live location. Digital sharing should be limited to people and services you trust; public posts can reveal where someone lives or travels. For a group, agree on a meeting point and a communication plan in case people separate. Accessibility and comfort are not bonus considerations: surfaces, lighting, seating, noise, crossing time, opening hours, and weather can determine whether the route is usable. A navigation app's fastest path is a calculation, not a judgment about personal safety, accessibility, legal access, or local closures. Don't cut through private property or closed land to save time. The most resilient plan includes a way to ask for help and permission to turn back, delay, or choose a safer alternative. Wayfinding is successful when everyone arrives safely, not when the intended route is followed at any cost.

TRY THIS

Prepare a small local trip.

For a familiar public destination, identify a current route, entrance, landmark, alternate path, and a place to pause safely. Check accessibility and weather with appropriate local sources; carry a backup contact plan, and change your plan if conditions differ from the map.

Safety first: This guide cannot provide route-specific navigation. Follow local alerts and trained outdoor guidance.
