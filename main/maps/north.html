MAPS & PLACES / DIRECTION

What does
“north” mean?

Three different norths can appear on a single map, and each answers a slightly different question.

THE MAIN IDEA

A compass, a grid, and the Earth axis do not quite align.

True north points along a meridian toward the geographic North Pole, which is defined by Earth's rotation axis. Grid north points toward the top of the map's projected coordinate grid. Magnetic north is the direction a magnetic compass needle aligns with Earth's magnetic field at a given place and time. These directions can differ because projections transform curved geography onto a flat grid, and Earth's magnetic field is not a perfect representation of the rotation axis. The angle between true north and magnetic north is called magnetic declination or magnetic variation; its direction and size vary across the planet and change over time as the magnetic field shifts. Grid convergence describes the difference between grid north and true north in a particular mapped region. A topographic map may include a declination diagram so a navigator can interpret bearings correctly. The diagram itself may be dated, so a decades-old paper map can show a magnetic value that no longer applies. A phone compass can also be affected by nearby metal, magnets, electronics, vehicle structures, or calibration errors. A map rotated on a screen may show “north” somewhere other than the top of the display, and a compass bearing must be interpreted relative to the chosen north. These differences are usually small in ordinary city-scale orientation, but a small angular error can compound over a long distance, creating a significant displacement. Professional surveying, aviation, marine navigation, and wilderness travel have procedures and instruments to handle the relevant reference systems. Beginners should not combine map bearings and compass readings based on memory alone. Check the current map diagram, follow a qualified instructor, and practise in a safe, familiar environment. Never attempt a hazardous journey based only on a phone compass and a web explanation. In an urban grid, “north” may colloquially mean along the city layout rather than geographic north; street names can also use local conventions. Asking which north is meant removes ambiguity. The broader lesson is that directional labels depend on reference frames, just like position coordinates and elevation datums. A symbol that looks universal can represent a location-dependent measurement. Reading the legend and date is not extra cartographic fuss; it is how the map tells you what its arrow actually points toward.

TRY THIS

Compare compass north with a map's grid.

Using a current local map and a compass in a safe open area, identify the declination notes before comparing a bearing. Treat the exercise as an orientation demonstration, not a navigation test; stay on familiar ground and follow local instructions.

Navigation training: Compass and map use in hazardous settings requires current local data, suitable equipment, and training.
