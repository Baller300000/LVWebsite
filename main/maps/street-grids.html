Find the logic in
a city street grid.

Urban blocks are geometry, history, and politics drawn across the ground.

THE MAIN IDEA

A grid is a pattern with practical exceptions.

A street grid organizes movement into a pattern of blocks and intersections. The simplest orthogonal grid has streets crossing at right angles, making directions predictable and allowing multiple route choices. But real cities rarely obey a perfect square. Terrain, rivers, shorelines, railways, historic property lines, older roads, and planning decisions bend or interrupt the pattern. Diagonal streets may follow an earlier path, aim toward a civic landmark, or connect areas more directly across a grid. Cul-de-sacs and pedestrian passages change who can travel through a block. One-way streets mean that a route's direction matters even when the map looks symmetrical. Street naming conventions differ among cities: avenues may run north–south in one place and east–west in another, and numbered streets can skip or restart across a boundary. A grid's apparent north may not match geographic north, which matters if you are trying to relate an address to a compass direction. High-density blocks and superblocks shape walking distance, traffic, shade, and the route options people have. A map legend can distinguish public streets, alleys, pedestrian-only paths, private roads, and restricted entrances, although map data can be incomplete or outdated. A short line across a block may be a stairway inaccessible to a wheelchair, a locked passage, or a private entrance. Accessibility is not a visual property one can safely infer from a basic road map; look for current verified information and contact the relevant service if a route is important. To understand a city, compare maps from different dates. A street alignment that appears illogical may make sense as a trace of a former shoreline, transit corridor, or annexed neighborhood. Historical patterns also reveal whose routes were privileged and which areas were divided by highways or zoning. Urban planning discussions should rely on local context and the voices of the people who use a place rather than treating a map as a neutral diagnostic. For a walking trip, check current crossings, traffic, construction, lighting, and public access. A grid can offer legibility and choice, but intersections add crossing exposure, and long blocks can make a nearby destination difficult to reach. Looking at the street pattern as a system turns an ordinary stroll into a chance to understand how design shapes everyday movement.

TRY THIS

Trace why one street breaks the grid.

Choose a familiar public district and compare its street map with an older or terrain map. Mark one diagonal street, interrupted block, or curve. List two plausible historical or geographic explanations, then look for reliable local evidence rather than guessing.
