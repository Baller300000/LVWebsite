BACKYARD NATURALIST / WATER

Follow one raindrop
through a watershed.

Water connects rooftops, gardens, gutters, streams, lakes, and coastlines into one landscape system.

THE MAIN IDEA

Ridges and gravity give a watershed its shape.

A watershed is the land area that drains precipitation toward a common outlet, such as a creek, wetland, lake, river, or section of coast. The boundaries are usually high ground or ridgelines: water falling on one side may flow to an entirely different river from water falling just over the divide. A raindrop can soak into the soil, remain in a puddle, evaporate, enter a drain, or travel through a sequence of channels before reaching a larger body of water. The path depends on slope, soil, vegetation, geology, buildings, storm infrastructure, and how wet the ground already is. In a forest or healthy wetland, roots and porous ground slow and store water; pavement and compacted surfaces can make runoff faster, creating higher flood peaks and carrying pollutants from streets into waterways. Urban storm drains may bypass natural filtration, while separate sanitary sewers and combined systems behave differently depending on a city's design. A map, contour lines, and local watershed information reveal hidden connections that casual observation misses. A hill you never notice from the road may separate two water systems, and an outfall visible on a shore may drain streets many blocks away. What enters a storm drain can affect people and wildlife downstream, so avoid putting waste, paint, chemicals, soil, or yard debris where rainfall might carry them into a gutter. Field observations should stay on safe, public ground; never enter storm drains, culverts, flood channels, or fast-flowing water. Following water after an ordinary light rain can be instructive, but flash floods are unpredictable and can become lethal rapidly. Check current local flood alerts and stay far away from flooded roads and channels. An introductory walk can reveal where water gathers and how vegetation buffers it, but it cannot establish whether a stream is safe to touch, fish, or drink. Testing and management decisions belong to local watershed professionals. Thinking in watersheds helps explain why responsible choices made far uphill can protect water that many other people and species depend on.

TRY THIS

Trace a route using public maps.

After a light rain, note safe puddles, gutters, and visible surface flow from a sidewalk. Compare those directions with a public topographic or watershed map to estimate where the water may ultimately drain. Do not approach an outfall, enter a drain, disturb streambanks, or sample water.

Flood safety: Never enter storm drains, floodwater, or fast-moving channels. Follow local flood warnings.
