BACKYARD NATURALIST / RECYCLING

What composting
can and cannot do.

Composting is microbial teamwork, not an instant disposal trick.

THE MAIN IDEA

Microbes need the right conditions and appropriate material.

Composting is the controlled decomposition of selected organic material by bacteria, fungi, and other decomposers. They use carbon-rich and nitrogen-rich material for energy and growth, while water and oxygen influence how efficiently the process proceeds. Dry leaves, shredded cardboard, and other “browns” provide a carbon source and a loose structure; fresh garden trimmings and certain food scraps provide more nitrogen and moisture. There is no single perfect recipe for every bin, because particle size, starting material, weather, container shape, and turning practices all differ. The pile should generally feel moist rather than dripping, and air spaces help limit unpleasant anaerobic conditions. If it smells rotten or stays soggy, add dry carbon-rich material and follow the instructions for your composting system. A cool household heap may not reach temperatures capable of destroying pathogens or weed seeds, so it should not be treated as a sterilization process. Local municipal rules determine what materials belong in a backyard bin or organics collection. Meat, dairy, cooked food, pet waste, diseased plants, invasive weeds, glossy packaging, treated wood, and bioplastics can require specialized processing or be prohibited entirely. Adding unsuitable scraps can attract rodents, contaminate finished compost, spread weeds, or create health risks. Composting is also not a replacement for reducing food waste in the first place; planning purchases, storing food properly, and using edible leftovers may conserve more effort and resources. Finished compost usually looks more uniform and earthy than the original scraps, but appearance alone does not guarantee its safety or maturity. Use it only in ways consistent with local advice, and never put it into sensitive waterways or habitat without expert guidance. A basic log of inputs, moisture, odor, and approximate temperature can teach you what your particular bin needs over time. Start with a small, permitted container rather than collecting material you do not know how to manage. Good composting is quiet, patient, and specific to its setting—not an excuse to label every biodegradable-looking object as garden fertilizer.

TRY THIS

Make a one-week input log.

Check your local collection rules first. For a permitted home bin, record each suitable input and whether the pile seems dry, damp, or soggy. Add dry leaves or shredded untreated paper when needed, following the bin manufacturer's advice; keep questionable materials out.

Follow local guidance: Home compost does not reliably sterilize materials. Check local rules about food, pests, and pathogens.
