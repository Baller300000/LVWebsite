BACKYARD NATURALIST / ASTRONOMY

Watch the Moon
keep time.

A handful of pencil sketches reveals the changing geometry of the Earth–Moon–Sun system.

THE MAIN IDEA

Moon phases are about viewpoint.

The Moon does not generate its own visible light; sunlight illuminates the side facing the Sun, while observers on Earth see different portions of that bright half as the Moon travels around our planet. When the Moon lies roughly between Earth and Sun, the side toward us is mostly dark and the phase is called new. Roughly a week later, a crescent grows toward the first-quarter phase; another week or so later the whole Earth-facing disk is illuminated at full Moon. Then the visible bright part shrinks through waning gibbous and crescent phases. The lunar cycle does not take exactly four neat seven-day weeks, so a calendar month and a lunar phase do not line up perfectly. At first quarter, the Moon is often high around sunset, whereas a full Moon generally rises near sunset and sets around sunrise; local horizon, season, and orbital tilt change the details. The curved boundary between lit and dark portions is called the terminator and can make craters and mountains especially visible through a small telescope. The Moon's appearance also depends on hemisphere: the illuminated side may look oriented differently from one place on Earth to another. A photograph taken through a phone camera can exaggerate brightness, so a sketch made with the naked eye is a perfectly respectable measurement. Clouds may interrupt your sequence; a gap does not invalidate it. Observations become more informative when you record date, time, direction, phase shape, and where the Moon sits relative to a familiar roofline. The Moon is a bright guide to the night sky, but sunlight is dangerous through magnifying optics. Never aim binoculars, a telescope, or a camera viewfinder near the Sun; proper solar viewing requires certified equipment installed exactly as intended. Start after sunset, from a safe place, and let the changing crescent prompt questions rather than trying to rush through the entire lunar calendar in one night.

TRY THIS

Keep a two-week Moon diary.

At roughly the same evening time, sketch the Moon's illuminated shape and apparent position each clear night. Record the date and direction, and mark cloudy nights honestly. Compare your sequence with a reliable lunar calendar; the changing illumination should become clearer than any single observation.

Eye safety: Never look at the Sun through binoculars, a telescope, or an unfiltered optical viewfinder.
