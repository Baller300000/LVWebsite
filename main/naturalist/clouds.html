BACKYARD NATURALIST / WEATHER

Name a cloud,
watch it change.

A cloud is a moment in moving air. Its transformation can be more interesting than its label.

THE MAIN IDEA

Clouds record conditions, but not the whole forecast.

Clouds form when moist air cools enough for water droplets or ice crystals to gather around tiny particles in the atmosphere. Their appearance reflects altitude, moisture, stability, and how air is moving, yet the sky seen from one yard offers only a partial picture of a much larger weather system. A high, thin veil, a broad gray layer, and an individual growing tower have different structures and can be described as different cloud genera, but names are useful tools rather than guarantees. Layered stratus clouds commonly cover a wide area. Cumulus clouds often have distinct puffy tops, especially when pockets of warmer air rise. Cirrus clouds form high and are composed mostly of ice crystals. Real skies contain blends and transitions: a flat cloud may break into patches, or growing convection may become vertically extensive. Wind at cloud level can differ from the breeze at your feet, so a cloud's drift is a clue about upper-level motion, not necessarily the surface forecast. Observe changes across time. Note the edge becoming sharper or softer, the cloud thickening, or neighboring formations moving at different speeds. Shade, buildings, mountains, temperature, and local sea breezes all influence what you see. A cloud chart can help develop vocabulary, while official meteorological forecasts combine instruments, radar, satellite data, and numerical models that are not available to a casual observer. Do not use cloud identification as the only basis for deciding whether a storm is safe. Thunder, official warnings, rapidly changing weather, and local emergency advice should take priority over a satisfying field sketch. If the sky is calm, a notebook and a sheltered viewpoint are enough for a small observational study. Comparing your notes with a forecast after the fact will gradually teach you which visible clues recur locally and which assumptions fail in your climate.

TRY THIS

Make a short sky sequence.

From a safe, sheltered place, sketch one part of the sky every ten minutes for half an hour. Record cloud height as low, middle, or high, apparent shape, direction of drift, and whether the coverage grows or breaks apart. Afterwards compare the sequence with a trusted local forecast; treat warnings as instructions, not as material for an experiment.

Weather safety: Use official forecasts and alerts, and seek shelter when dangerous conditions threaten.
