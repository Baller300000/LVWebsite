CREATIVE WORKSHOP / STORY

A story moves when
something changes.

A character's choice gives a scene direction, even when the story is quiet and very short.

THE MAIN IDEA

Desire and consequence create movement.

A story is not merely a list of events. It is an arranged experience in which an audience learns what matters, watches a change unfold, and interprets the consequences. Begin with someone who wants something specific. The desire can be small: get across town before the library closes, apologize without sounding defensive, or find out who left a light on. Add an obstacle that makes the attempt difficult, and a choice that reveals what the character values. The result may be success, failure, compromise, or an unexpected understanding, but it should change what the character can do next. A dramatic explosion is not required. A quiet story can turn on noticing a new fact or deciding not to send a message. Scenes become vivid through concrete details and behavior rather than long explanations. Instead of telling us a character is anxious, show how they rehearse a simple sentence and then replace it. Choose details that let the reader infer meaning without withholding so much information that the scene becomes confusing. Point of view controls what the audience knows. A first-person narrator cannot report another character's private thoughts without evidence, while a close third-person perspective can stay near one character's perceptions. Switching viewpoints needs clear signals and a reason. Dialogue works when each speaker has a distinct purpose; real conversation contains filler, while fictional dialogue is selected. Avoid giving every character the author's best prepared speech. Conflict does not have to mean hostility. Two people can share a goal but disagree about timing, methods, or risks. A story outline is a tool, not a promise that every scene must be written in order. Drafting a scene out of sequence may reveal its heart before you know how to get there. Keep the scope suitable to the form: a flash fiction piece may center on one turning point, while a novel can support many changing goals. Revise by asking what the reader needs to understand at each moment, then test whether the character's action makes sense given what they know. If a choice feels arbitrary, show the pressure or missing information that makes it plausible. Once a draft has room to breathe, invite a reader to describe what they understood—not just whether they liked it. Their confusion can point to a missing beat; their disagreement can simply be evidence that people interpret stories differently. The aim is a shaped experience, not a formula assembled from mandatory tropes.

TRY THIS

Write a tiny change without an epic battle.

Write 150 words about a character trying to complete an ordinary task before a small deadline. Give them one obstacle, one choice, and a changed understanding or outcome. Underline the moment they decide and check that the obstacle is visible on the page.
