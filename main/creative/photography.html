CREATIVE WORKSHOP / PHOTOGRAPHY

Take a better photograph
by slowing down.

A phone camera is already enough to practise the most important photographic decision: what the picture is for.

THE MAIN IDEA

Find the subject before touching the controls.

A photograph is an arrangement of light at a particular moment. Before changing exposure or switching lenses, decide what you want the viewer to notice. Move a step to one side and the background may become simpler; lower the camera and the foreground may frame the subject; wait a moment and an expression or cloud may change. The edges matter as much as the center. Watch for branches apparently growing from a person's head, a bright sign pulling attention away, or a horizon cutting through the subject. Compose to include context when place matters, or simplify when detail is the story. Light determines both visibility and mood. Soft light from cloud cover reduces harsh shadows, while direct sunlight creates stronger contrast and may hide detail in the darkest areas. A phone's automatic exposure can change the look when a bright sky enters the frame. Tap exposure controls carefully and inspect highlights and shadows rather than assuming the brightest image is the most accurate. Zooming digitally may crop and enlarge pixels, reducing detail; moving closer is not always appropriate or safe, so respect distance and privacy. Stabilize the camera, especially in low light, and take a moment to make sure the lens is clean. A tripod and special camera are optional, not the source of a photograph's idea. Color and image processing affect interpretation. If a photo documents science, history, an event, or a product, avoid edits that misrepresent what was observed, and preserve the original where accuracy matters. For portraits, ask for informed permission before taking or sharing someone's image, particularly a child. Respect site rules, sacred places, wildlife buffer distances, and private property. Taking a picture of an animal by approaching its nest or baiting it is not a harmless composition trick. One clear frame is often stronger than many random images. After shooting, review what attracts your eye first, whether the subject has enough breathing room, and whether another angle would tell the story more honestly. A useful practice is to take three frames with a single deliberate change between each—framing, distance, or timing—then compare them. You will learn how small decisions shape an image, and you will make better photographs with whatever camera happens to be in your pocket.

TRY THIS

Tell one small story in three frames.

Choose a safe, non-private subject and take three photographs: a wide context, a medium view, and a close detail. Change your position without disturbing the subject. Compare which frame explains the story most clearly, and ask permission before sharing recognizable people.

Consent matters: Follow privacy laws, location rules, and wildlife guidance; ask permission before sharing portraits.
