CREATIVE WORKSHOP / IMAGE MAKING

Collage:
make the cut visible.

A collage can create new meaning from fragments, but the fragments still have sources and people behind them.

THE MAIN IDEA

Selection and juxtaposition are both creative decisions.

Collage combines existing materials—photographs, text, illustration, textures, or found paper—into a new composition. The meaning arises partly from what each fragment shows and partly from the relationships created by its placement. Two images next to one another can suggest comparison, contrast, or a story that neither image held alone. Begin with an idea or question, then collect material that contributes to it instead of treating a pile of unrelated scraps as an automatic composition. Try arranging paper fragments without attaching them. Moving one piece may change the focal point, the implied chronology, and who appears to have power within the frame. Scale, crop, color, edge, repetition, and white space help a collage cohere. Repeated shapes can create a visual rhythm; one contrasting fragment can redirect attention. A visible cut may be part of the artwork, while a carefully blended edge may suggest a different relationship. These choices deserve reflection when the source includes people, historical images, or documentary evidence. Transformative juxtaposition can still imply a false claim about what a person did or said. Do not fabricate documentary evidence or deceptively edit a real event. For public or commercial use, copyright rules vary by jurisdiction, and “found on the internet” is not a reuse license. Obtain permission or use material with an appropriate license; retain source, creator, and attribution details. A collage that includes recognizable people or private correspondence raises privacy and consent concerns even where copyright permission exists. Public-domain status should be verified for the specific item and edition, since rights can differ between an original work and a digitized reproduction. Respect cultural protocols, sacred images, and communities whose artifacts were collected without consent. Your own photographs and drawings give you more control, but the depicted people may still need to consent before publication. When you use a digital app, check that it does not automatically upload private source images or claim rights you did not intend to grant. Show drafts to a viewer and ask what story the arrangement appears to tell. If their interpretation differs from your intention, refine the connection or add context; do not assume that your caption overrides a misleading image. Collage is most compelling when its visible fragments invite discovery and its creator can explain both the new composition and the responsibility carried by every borrowed piece.

TRY THIS

Collage from material you made yourself.

Cut or digitally combine scraps from your own notes, drawings, or photos of everyday objects. Make one clear visual comparison and one deliberate area of quiet. Record each source and ask a viewer what relationship they see.

Rights and context: Check copyright, licenses, consent, privacy, and cultural protocols before publishing source material.
