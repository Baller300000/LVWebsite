CREATIVE WORKSHOP / PHOTOGRAPHY

Look at the light
before the subject.

Light reveals a subject, hides it, colors it, and changes while you are deciding what to frame.

THE MAIN IDEA

Direction and softness change shape.

Light is not just a quantity for an exposure meter. Its direction determines which surfaces face the source and where shadows appear. Front light tends to flatten visible texture, side light emphasizes shape, and backlight can outline an object or make translucent materials glow. These are not quality rankings; the useful direction depends on the story. The apparent size of a light source relative to the subject influences shadow softness. A large nearby window can create soft transitions; a small distant lamp produces sharper ones. A cloud acts as a diffuser for the Sun, spreading daylight more evenly, while direct sun may create bright highlights and deep shadow in the same scene. Reflective walls, snow, water, and colored surfaces bounce light back with their own tone, changing how shadows look. The color temperature of morning, midday, indoor bulbs, and sunset differs; cameras may automatically adjust white balance, while a painter can intentionally exaggerate or neutralize the color cast. Automatic correction is not always the right choice if preserving the mood is important. Exposure determines how much recorded brightness is placed on the image, but recovering detail is limited: a clipped highlight may contain no recorded information, and a deep shadow may become noisy if brightened afterward. On a phone, tap to expose for the subject and inspect the entire frame; the preview may change when a bright sky appears at its edge. Artificial lights can add unwanted flicker or mixed color. Be cautious with flash, especially around people, wildlife, vehicles, reflective surfaces, or sites where photography is restricted. Do not point powerful lights at drivers, aircraft, or nocturnal animals. You can learn a lot without equipment by moving a safe object near a window, observing how its shadow shifts, and photographing it at different times from the same position. Keep camera settings stable if you are comparing. A scene that seems dull at noon may have beautiful long shadows at another hour; the illumination is a compositional ingredient that you can observe before deciding on a lens or subject. Notice where the light comes from, what bounces it, and what remains outside its reach. That small pause makes later edits more intentional and images more legible.

TRY THIS

Photograph the same object by a window.

Use a harmless still subject at a fixed position. Take one image with light from the front, one from the side, and one backlit, without moving the camera or object. Compare shadows and surface detail; do not use bright light to disturb people or animals.

Light safety: Never point bright lights or lasers at aircraft, drivers, wildlife, or a person's eyes.
