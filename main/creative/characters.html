CREATIVE WORKSHOP / CHARACTERS

Build a character
through choices.

A biography tells you who someone is said to be; the decisions on the page make that person legible.

THE MAIN IDEA

Specific behavior beats a stack of adjectives.

A fictional character feels particular when their actions emerge from a recognizable mix of wants, habits, knowledge, pressures, and contradictions. A list—brave, funny, stubborn—can give a writer private shorthand, but readers cannot see those labels directly. Show the character deciding whether to tell an uncomfortable truth, who they call when a plan fails, what they do with a promise that becomes inconvenient. Choices create evidence, and consequences reveal whether a choice mattered. A character can want to be generous yet resent the cost; that tension is often more interesting than a neat moral label. Background can explain how a character learned a habit, but history should not mechanically predict every future action. People change, remain inconsistent, and respond differently in different relationships. Give characters knowledge appropriate to their experience; a baker may notice dough texture that an outsider misses, but they are not automatically an expert in unrelated science. Distinct voices come from priorities, vocabulary, rhythm, and what each person avoids saying, not from giving everyone an accent written in difficult phonetic spellings. Be careful with identity and experience outside your own. Research first-person accounts, use qualified readers where appropriate, and avoid treating one person as the representative of an entire group. A character can carry stereotypes even when the author intends sympathy. Give supporting characters agency and goals that are not merely tools to develop the protagonist. Let them surprise the central figure for reasons that make sense in their own lives. Small behavior is useful: what someone notices in a room, how they handle borrowed property, whether they listen, and when they change the subject can reveal more than a monologue about their values. Character sheets can help organize continuity, but a character is not finished when every box is filled. Ask what the person wants in this scene, what they risk by acting, what they believe about the other person, and how new information could change their mind. Then revise dialogue and movement so those answers become visible. A convincing character need not be likable; they need to be understandable within the story's context. Readers can disagree with a choice while recognizing the pressure that produced it. That gap between empathy and approval gives fiction room for difficult questions. Treat identity and private details with respect, and do not copy a real person's vulnerable story without permission.

TRY THIS

Reveal a value through a decision.

Write a one-page scene where a character can keep a small convenience or honor a promise. Do not name their trait. Show the choice, what they give up, and how another person responds. Ask a reader which value they inferred and which detail supported it.

Representation: Research responsibly and avoid treating a character as a stand-in for an entire identity group.
