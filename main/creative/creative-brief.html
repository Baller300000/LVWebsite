CREATIVE WORKSHOP / STARTING POINT

A creative brief
for one person.

A few useful constraints can make an open-ended idea easier to start.

THE MAIN IDEA

Describe the job before polishing the artifact.

A creative brief is a short description of what a project is trying to accomplish, who it is for, and what boundaries it needs to respect. It is not a contract or a test of seriousness. For a personal project, one paragraph and a handful of bullet points can prevent weeks of polishing a result that does not answer the original question. Start with a purpose: what should a viewer, reader, or listener learn, feel, or be able to do? Describe the audience concretely without reducing people to stereotypes. “A person who has never used this site and is trying it on a small phone” offers more useful design direction than “everyone.” Choose a medium and define the deliverable: one poster, a three-minute audio piece, a small web page, or a folded eight-page zine. Write the limitations down. Available time, tools, budget, color use, image permissions, language, accessibility, and production method all shape the result. Constraints can help focus creativity, but unsafe or impossible conditions should be revisited instead of treated as a heroic challenge. Define a finish line in observable terms: the page loads on a narrow screen, the text can be read aloud, the print fits a chosen sheet, or a second person can follow the instructions. Avoid defining success only as “make it beautiful” or “make everyone love it”; those outcomes are subjective and outside the creator's control. Include a “not this” line to protect the project's scope. For instance, a one-page local guide is not a complete global database. Add a question to investigate rather than pretending it already has an answer. Read the brief again after making a rough prototype. If the project has changed, update the brief to explain why, instead of feeling obliged to keep an outdated plan. A brief is a navigation tool, not a cage; evidence and audience feedback can change it. Keeping the final brief beside the finished piece makes it easier to understand whether the design decisions fit the original purpose. The act of choosing a small, specific outcome is itself creative work. It gives the first draft permission to exist and helps you notice when it has done enough to be complete.

TRY THIS

Fill one index card, then begin.

Write five short prompts: I am making; it is for; it should help them; I have this much time and these tools; it is finished when. Make one rough draft that fits the brief, then change the card only after noticing a concrete reason.
