CREATIVE WORKSHOP / FEEDBACK

Ask for feedback
you can actually use.

“What do you think?” is a hard question when neither person knows what kind of response is welcome.

THE MAIN IDEA

A focused question produces a focused conversation.

Feedback helps a creator compare their intention with another person's experience. It is not a vote on whether the creator has talent, and it does not need to be accepted as an instruction. Before sharing a draft, give the reader brief context: what the piece is for, who is meant to encounter it, and which stage it has reached. A rough structure benefits from different questions than a nearly finished layout. Ask about one area at a time. “Where did you lose the thread?” is more actionable than “Is it good?” “Can you find the booking link on a phone?” can uncover an observable problem. Let the person describe their experience before explaining every choice; early explanations can accidentally steer their response. Listen for what they did, what they expected, and where they hesitated. Then ask a follow-up question to understand the reason. A reader's interpretation is evidence about one reader in one context, not a measurement of every possible audience. Patterns across several appropriate readers may reveal a common issue; a single opinion may reveal an important edge case or simply a preference. Ethical feedback begins with consent: the reader should know what they are being asked to do, how their words might be shared, and whether their identity or work will remain private. Creators should also respect boundaries and not demand unpaid labor from people whose expertise they need. If you are asking for accessibility or cultural review, compensate qualified consultants when possible and avoid treating one participant as a representative of an entire group. Feedback providers should distinguish an observation (“I could not tell which button was selected”) from a prescription (“make everything orange”). You can learn from the former and decide how to respond to the latter. After the conversation, write down the feedback in your own words and separate recurring evidence, new questions, and personal taste. Wait before making major revisions if you feel overwhelmed. You remain responsible for the final choices, including factual accuracy, copyright, accessibility, and the safety of the work's audience. A clear request protects the reader's time, the creator's agency, and the project's actual goal. It also gives the discussion a kind place to be honest: criticize the design decision, not the person who made it.

TRY THIS

Ask someone to do one real task.

Show a draft to a willing reader, state the intended audience and context, and ask them to complete one realistic task while thinking aloud. Observe without coaching, thank them, and make one testable revision. Do not publish their feedback without permission.

Respect boundaries: Ask permission, protect privacy, and value specialist or lived-experience expertise.
