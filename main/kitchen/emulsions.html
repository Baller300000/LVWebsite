KITCHEN NOTEBOOK / MIXING

Why oil and water
sometimes stay together.

A shaken dressing is not a new substance; it is a temporary arrangement of tiny droplets.

THE MAIN IDEA

An emulsion is a dispersal with a stability problem.

Oil and water do not naturally mix into a stable uniform solution because their molecules interact differently. Shake or whisk them vigorously, however, and one liquid can break into small droplets dispersed through the other. The result is an emulsion. It may look creamy because the droplets scatter light, but it is usually temporary: droplets collide, merge, and eventually separate into visible layers. Smaller droplets and a thicker surrounding liquid can slow this process. An emulsifier helps by interacting with both oil and water, stabilizing the boundary around each droplet. In a vinaigrette, mustard can provide emulsifying material and viscosity. In mayonnaise, egg yolk contains emulsifiers that help oil disperse into an aqueous mixture; adding the oil gradually while mixing makes the initial droplets manageable. Temperature and ingredient order influence whether the emulsion forms, and over-aggressive additions can overwhelm it. Different recipes rely on different balances, so a stable sauce cannot be made by assuming that all fats and liquids behave identically. An emulsion can sometimes be repaired by starting with a small amount of water or yolk in a clean bowl and slowly whisking in the split mixture, but success depends on the recipe and how it failed. A simple oil-and-water jar is a useful visual demonstration, not a substitute for food safety: ingredients like raw egg require proper handling and storage. Plant-based emulsifiers exist too, and many packaged sauces use specific stabilizers selected for product quality. Cream, milk, and other everyday foods contain naturally structured mixtures, which is one reason they do not behave exactly like oil poured into a glass of water. Observe the droplets and time rather than treating “mixed” as a permanent state. A dressing that separates after sitting has not mysteriously spoiled just because the layers returned; conversely, appearance alone never proves food is safe. Refrigerate and discard prepared foods according to current guidance, and avoid tasting ingredients used only for a demonstration. Understanding droplet formation helps make salad dressing, sauces, and baking mixtures less mysterious, while reminding us that good technique and sound storage are separate questions.

TRY THIS

Watch a simple dressing separate.

In a closed, unbreakable jar, combine a little oil and water, shake briefly, and observe how quickly layers return. Repeat with a small amount of prepared mustard, then compare. Do not add raw egg or taste the demonstration; clean the jar afterward.

Food handling: Keep egg-based sauces refrigerated and follow current food-safety guidance.
