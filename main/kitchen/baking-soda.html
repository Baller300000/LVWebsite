KITCHEN NOTEBOOK / CHEMISTRY

Baking soda
is not baking powder.

Both help some baked goods rise, but they do not do the same job in the same way.

THE MAIN IDEA

Leavening makes gas; a batter has to hold on to it.

Baking soda is sodium bicarbonate, a chemical leavener that releases carbon dioxide when it reacts with acid and moisture; heating also contributes to its decomposition. Carbon dioxide forms bubbles that can expand a batter in the oven, but those bubbles only help if the mixture has enough structure to hold them. Baking powder combines a bicarbonate with one or more dry acids and usually a starch to keep the mixture stable. Some powders react partly when they are moistened and partly when heated, providing lift over more than one stage. Because baking soda relies on an acid source in the recipe, it is commonly used with ingredients such as cultured dairy, acidic fruit, cocoa processed by certain methods, or vinegar. The quantity and timing matter: excess bicarbonate may leave a soapy, bitter flavor, affect browning, or change the texture. Baking powder is formulated to contain acid as well as base, but its strength and double-acting behavior depend on the product. A substitution is therefore a change to a recipe's chemistry, not a swap of two identical white powders. Adding lemon juice to force a reaction can also add flavor and water that were not part of the tested formula. Leaveners lose effectiveness over time or when exposed to moisture, which is why a container's storage directions and date are worth checking. A recipe can also rise through air beaten into eggs or fat, steam expanding in the oven, or gas from yeast; these methods create different structure and flavor. A flat cake is not necessarily evidence that “more powder” is the answer. Overmixing, old leavener, incorrect oven temperature, a delayed bake, or a pan that is too large can each matter. Follow a tested formula first, then change one factor at a time if you are learning. Chemical demonstrations are educational, but household products must stay labeled, away from children and pets, and never be mixed indiscriminately for cleaning. Treat a written recipe as a carefully balanced system whose bubbles, acidity, and structure all work together.

TRY THIS

Compare bubbles with water.

In labeled, small cups, compare a pinch of baking soda in plain water and in a food-safe acidic liquid such as lemon juice. Observe bubbling from a safe distance. Do not seal the vessels or drink the mixture; clean up and store ingredients safely.

Kitchen safety: Follow a tested food recipe; this demonstration is not an edible recipe or chemical safety test.
