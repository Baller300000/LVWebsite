KITCHEN NOTEBOOK / TEMPERATURE

Heat is the ingredient
you cannot see.

A burner setting describes a control input; it does not tell you what is happening everywhere inside the pan.

THE MAIN IDEA

Heat moves from one place to another.

When food cooks, energy travels through direct contact, moving fluid, and electromagnetic radiation. A metal pan touching food transfers heat mainly by conduction. Hot water, oil, or air circulates and carries energy by convection. The flame, heating element, oven walls, or glowing coals also radiate energy to nearby surfaces. These routes happen together, but not at equal speed. A thick pan can hold considerable heat and may recover slowly after cold ingredients are added. A lightweight pan responds quickly but can form hot and cool patches above an uneven burner. Water on a wet surface must evaporate before that surface can get substantially hotter, so crowded ingredients and a puddle can prevent the dry sizzling conditions needed for strong browning. A burner label such as “medium” is relative to one cooker and one pan; surface temperature also depends on the time spent preheating, room conditions, pan material, and the ingredients' initial temperature. A thermometer gives one measurement in one place at one moment, not the complete internal history of a large piece of food. In a roast, surface color says little about the center; for food safety, use authoritative guidance for the particular food and a suitable thermometer inserted as instructed. Changing only one factor—pan material, moisture, quantity, or preheat time—makes it easier to understand why a result changes. Heat also keeps travelling after the pan leaves the stove because the outside of the food may be hotter than its center. That stored energy can continue cooking the interior while the exterior cools. A recipe's resting time is therefore part of the cooking process, not decorative waiting. Knowing the routes that energy takes can explain browning, sogginess, uneven texture, and carryover without relying on the vague instruction to “turn it up.” Small controlled adjustments and attention to the food itself are more useful than a universal burner number.

TRY THIS

Compare pan recovery, safely.

With an adult where appropriate, compare two small batches of the same ingredient in different pans. Keep burner, ingredient amount, and time constant; note sound and color. Avoid touching hot surfaces, and never leave active cooking unattended.

Heat safety: Use dry oven mitts, turn handles inward, and follow temperature guidance for any food needing safe internal cooking.
