KITCHEN NOTEBOOK / EXTRACTION

A cup of coffee is
a little extraction lab.

Brewing is water carrying soluble material from ground coffee, one changing cup at a time.

THE MAIN IDEA

Extraction depends on contact and flow.

Ground coffee contains many soluble compounds that dissolve into water at different rates. Brewing moves water through or around coffee, and the changing concentration, temperature, grind, time, and agitation influence what ends up in the cup. Finer grinding exposes more surface area and often speeds extraction, while coarse particles can slow it; however, a particular grind must fit the brewing method because an espresso machine and a French press handle flow very differently. A filter that drains too slowly may reflect fine particles, a clogged bed, or pouring technique, not one universal fault. The amount of coffee relative to water changes strength: strength is the concentration in the drink, whereas extraction is how much material has dissolved from the coffee. A strong cup is not automatically over-extracted, and a weak one is not necessarily under-extracted. Water chemistry matters too; minerals influence how coffee compounds dissolve, while very hard, soft, or off-flavored water can change the result. Freshly roasted beans release gas after roasting and evolve with time, but the best resting and storage practices depend on roast and packaging. Keep beans sealed from air, moisture, heat, and strong odors. Weighing beans and water makes comparison easier than relying on scoops, since bean size and grind density vary. If a cup tastes unpleasant, alter one controllable factor and keep the rest steady: adjust grind slightly, use a consistent ratio, or check brew time. Tasting notes are subjective descriptions, not chemicals you must be able to detect. A person may notice cocoa-like, citrus-like, or floral impressions while someone else tastes simply “coffee,” and neither experience is a failed test. The equipment is not a requirement for enjoying a cup; a simple immersion method can teach the same basic idea. Hot water can burn and brewing equipment retains heat, so work carefully and keep cords and glassware secure. The experiment is a repeatable cup, not a perfect score. Writing down what you changed turns preference into useful knowledge about your own beans and method.

TRY THIS

Adjust only the grind.

With the same beans, water mass, brew method, and contact time, make two small cups with adjacent grind settings if your grinder permits. Record the ratio and taste after cooling enough to drink safely. A difference does not prove one setting is universally better.

Hot-liquid safety: Secure filters and handles, and take care around boiling water and heated equipment.
