KITCHEN NOTEBOOK / BREWING

Tea, time,
temperature, and taste.

A tea label gives you a starting point; your water, cup, and palate complete the experiment.

THE MAIN IDEA

Infusion is extraction that changes over time.

Tea is made by allowing soluble compounds from plant material to move into water. The result depends on leaf size, processing, the amount of tea, water temperature, contact time, and the water itself. Tiny particles have more exposed surface area and can release compounds quickly; larger rolled leaves may unfurl during a longer infusion. Hotter water generally speeds extraction, but the flavor balance and preferred temperature depend on the kind of tea and personal taste. Some teas become pleasantly fuller with additional steeping; others become more bitter or astringent because different compounds enter the water at different rates. A recommended time is a helpful baseline from the producer, not a universal law that every cup must obey. Green, white, oolong, black, and dark teas are broad families made through different cultivars and processing, and individual teas within one family still behave differently. Water that tastes strongly of chlorine or minerals can mask delicate aromas. A clean kettle and fresh drinking water make sensory comparisons easier, though local water safety always comes first. Tea leaves may be re-infused depending on style, but an old wet leaf is a perishable food product; follow packaging and safe storage guidance rather than leaving it warm for days. A small kitchen scale can make comparisons more repeatable, but a spoon is enough to begin noticing differences if you record the type of leaf and dose. Drink at a safe temperature; freshly boiled liquid can scald even when it looks harmless in a cup. Personal descriptions are welcome. One taster might find a tea floral; another may focus on its toasted, grassy, fruity, or pleasantly plain quality. These are observations, not mistakes. To find your own preference, alter one brewing variable at a time and cool each sample to a comparable temperature before evaluating it. Do not assume that bitterness means the tea is poor: steeping time, water temperature, leaf ratio, and storage all interact. A carefully recorded side-by-side cup gives you more information than blindly memorizing a chart. The useful outcome is learning how you enjoy that leaf in your conditions, then repeating the method.

TRY THIS

Make a two-cup comparison.

Divide one measured tea portion between two cups with equal water volumes. Steep one for a shorter time and the other for a longer time, using the producer's recommended temperature. Cool them enough to sip safely, then compare aroma, bitterness, and body without trying to name every flavor.

Scald prevention: Pour boiling water carefully and keep hot kettles beyond the reach of children and pets.
