THE MAIN IDEA

Find the exact rule that creates the contradiction.

A paradox is not merely a surprising answer or a puzzle that takes a long time. It is a situation in which reasoning from apparently acceptable premises reaches a contradiction, or a claim whose truth cannot be settled within the rules being used. Many familiar paradoxes arise from self-reference: a sentence refers to its own truth, creating a loop in which calling it true makes it false and calling it false makes it true. Others reveal that a word was used in two different senses, a boundary was never defined, or a physical model was extended past where its assumptions hold. Begin by writing the premises as short statements. Identify what each key word means, including whether a group is finite, what counts as an object in it, and whether the statement is about a model or the real world. Then follow the inference one step at a time, asking which rule licenses each move. A sorites puzzle asks how many grains make a heap, but that question depends on a vague category with no crisp boundary. If removing one grain never changes heap status, repeated removals seem to imply a single grain is still a heap; the problem exposes the limits of a gradual-change premise, not a secret hidden pile. The liar sentence “this sentence is false” explores the complications self-referential truth can create. It is not repaired merely by shouting “false”; one must specify what languages and truth predicates the formal system permits. Mathematical and computer-science paradoxes have helped motivate rigorous definitions, type distinctions, and formal systems that block certain loops. In everyday arguments, a paradoxical-looking result may come from incompatible goals: a vote can be fair under one criterion but unfair under another, for example. More precise assumptions may resolve the apparent contradiction, while others reveal a genuine limitation that deserves further study. Do not assume every headline calling something a paradox presents a formal paradox; it may just be counterintuitive or statistically rare. Draw a table of the statements and their dependencies. If the chain loops, mark the step that refers back to the starting point. If a term shifts meaning, rewrite it with the intended meaning fixed. The goal is not to force every puzzle into a tidy answer, but to identify whether the difficulty lies in the rules, the language, the evidence, or a real unresolved tension.

TRY THIS

Trace a circular claim by sentence.

Choose a harmless self-referential sentence from a logic text. Write what follows if it is true, then what follows if it is false. Identify precisely where the definitions of “true” and “false” depend on the sentence itself; do not import unstated assumptions.
